What to Check Before Buying a Lot in Winter Park for a Custom Home

October 26, 2026

Before you buy a lot in Winter Park to build a custom home, settle six questions. What does the zoning allow? How much of the lot can you actually build on once setbacks and other limits apply? Which utilities serve the property? What is the soil like? How exposed is the lot to flooding? Does the neighborhood fit the home you want? If you can answer all six with confidence, you're buying land. If you can't, you're buying a guess.

The answers come from the city or county that governs the parcel, a survey, a soil report and a good look at the street. Get them before you close, while you can still walk away or renegotiate. The rest of this article covers each question, where to find the answer, and why it matters for a ground-up custom home.

Why the Lot Comes First

A lot sets the limits of your home before anyone draws a line. It decides the footprint, how close you can build to the property lines, where the driveway goes, how the house faces the sun and how much site work comes before the foundation.

In Winter Park this matters even more. Much of the city is built out, so many lots that come up for sale already have an older house on them. Others sit on or near the chain of lakes. Some have mature oaks the city may protect. Each of those situations has its own questions, and a lot that looks like a bargain on paper can get expensive once they're answered.

1. Zoning: What the City Allows

Start with the zoning designation. It tells you what can be built and sets the basic limits on size and placement.

Winter Park is its own city with its own land development code, separate from the City of Orlando and unincorporated Orange County. A Winter Park mailing address doesn't guarantee the parcel is inside city limits. Some sit in unincorporated Orange County, and some may fall in a neighboring county. Confirm which jurisdiction governs the parcel before you assume anything.

Questions to ask the planning staff:

  • What is the zoning district for this parcel?
  • What are the limits on building height, lot coverage and floor area?
  • Are there extra rules for lakefront property, historic areas or overlay districts?
  • Are there tree protection rules that affect where the house can go?
  • If there's an existing house, what's involved in getting a demolition permit?

Rules change, and summaries online go out of date. Get current answers straight from the city or county that governs the parcel, and write down who you spoke with and when.

For parcels in Orange County, you can look up the basic record, including ownership, lot dimensions and property use, through the Orange County Property Appraiser. If the parcel is in another county, use that county's property appraiser. It's a good first step, but it doesn't replace a zoning check.

2. Setbacks and the Buildable Area

A 100-foot-wide lot doesn't give you 100 feet of house. Setbacks set how far the structure has to sit from the front, side and rear property lines. On lakefront lots there may be extra limits near the water.

Once you take out setbacks, easements, protected trees and any required open space, what's left is your buildable envelope. That envelope is what really matters.

Get a Current Survey

Ask for a recent boundary survey, or order one. A survey shows:

  • Exact property lines and lot dimensions
  • Recorded easements for utilities or drainage
  • Encroachments such as fences, driveways or structures crossing the line
  • Existing structures and improvements

A topographic survey adds elevation data, which helps with drainage and flood questions later.

Check the Title and Deed Restrictions

Some Winter Park neighborhoods have deed restrictions or HOA covenants on top of city zoning. They can cover home size, architectural style, materials, fencing and more. Read them before you close. If an HOA is involved, ask for its current governing documents and any architectural review process. Don't assume what's allowed. Ask the HOA directly.

3. Utilities: What's Already There

Utilities are easy to overlook and costly to add later. For each lot, find out:

  • Water: Who provides service, and is there a connection at the property?
  • Sewer or septic: Is the lot on central sewer, or would it need a septic system? Septic brings its own permitting and site requirements.
  • Electric: Who is the provider, and where is the nearest service point?
  • Gas: Is natural gas available on the street, if you want it?
  • Stormwater: How does the lot drain now, and where does the water go?

Service providers can differ around the Winter Park area depending on exactly where the parcel sits. Call the providers with the parcel address and ask what's available and what connecting would involve. If an older house is coming down, ask how the existing service lines will be handled.

4. Soil: What's Under the Surface

Central Florida soil varies a lot, even over short distances. Sandy soil is common, but so are pockets of organic material (muck), clay layers and high water tables. Parts of the region also have sinkhole activity.

None of that has to rule a lot out. It does affect how the foundation is designed and what the site work involves. A geotechnical investigation, usually soil borings by a licensed engineer, tells you what you're building on.

Questions a soil report helps answer:

  • Is the soil suitable for a standard foundation, or does it need extra preparation?
  • How high is the water table?
  • Is there buried debris from an old structure, especially on teardown lots?
  • Are there signs of subsurface voids or unstable layers?

Ask whether you can make the purchase contingent on acceptable soil results. A short delay before closing costs far less than a foundation surprise after.

5. Flood Exposure and Storm Readiness

Winter Park's lakes are a big part of its appeal. Water also brings risk. Even lots that aren't on the water can sit low or collect runoff.

Check the lot's flood zone designation on the current FEMA flood maps and ask your insurance agent what that designation means for coverage. Ask the city or county about any local floodplain requirements, such as minimum finished floor elevations. Look at the lot after a heavy rain if you can. Standing water tells you something no map will.

Central Florida homes also have to be built for wind. The Florida Building Code sets statewide construction standards, including wind resistance. Your builder and design team handle compliance, but knowing the code exists helps you ask better questions about how your home will be built.

For storm preparation and hazard information, the Florida Division of Emergency Management is a useful starting point.

6. Neighborhood Fit

This one isn't on a permit application, but it matters as much as any of the others.

A custom home should fit its street. That doesn't mean copying the houses next door. It means understanding the context. Walk the neighborhood at different times of day. Notice:

  • The scale and style of nearby homes
  • Traffic, noise and how close you are to busy roads
  • How the lot faces, and where the morning and afternoon sun fall
  • Views you'd want to frame and views you'd want to screen
  • Distance to the places you go every day

Think about how the home you want will sit on this particular lot. A design built around a long lakefront view needs a different lot than one built around a private courtyard.

A Quick Pre-Closing Checklist

Before you commit, try to have each of these in hand:

  1. Confirmed jurisdiction and zoning designation from the city or county
  2. Setback requirements and a rough buildable envelope
  3. A current boundary survey, and ideally a topographic survey
  4. Title review, including deed restrictions and HOA documents
  5. Confirmed utility availability and connection requirements
  6. A geotechnical soil report
  7. The flood zone designation and an insurance conversation
  8. Tree, demolition and lakefront requirements, if they apply
  9. Your own notes from walking the neighborhood

If something on the list is still open, think about whether your contract gives you time to close it.

Talk to a Builder Before You Close

Most of what's above is easier to judge with a builder in the conversation. A builder can look at a survey and tell you whether the home you have in mind fits. They can point out site work that might affect your budget, flag questions for the city before they become problems, and help you see whether a lot works for your plans.

That conversation is most useful before closing, while you still have options. After closing, the lot is yours, limits and all.

Your Next Step

Pull the parcel record for the lot you're considering on the Orange County Property Appraiser site, or that of the county where the parcel sits. Then call the planning department for the jurisdiction that governs it, usually the City of Winter Park, to confirm the zoning and setbacks. With those two answers you'll know whether the lot is worth a survey, a soil report and a conversation with a builder.
