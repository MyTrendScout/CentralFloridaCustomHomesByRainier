Check a Custom Home Builder's License in Florida Before You Sign

October 25, 2026

To check a custom home builder's license in Florida, go to the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) at myfloridalicense.com. Use the license search and look up the builder by name or license number. Then confirm three things: the license is current and active, it is the right type for building a home, and the name on it matches the person or company you are about to hire.

It takes a few minutes and costs nothing. Do it before you sign anything or put down a deposit. Then ask the builder a few direct questions the state database won't answer. Here is how to do both.

Why the License Check Matters

A custom home is likely the largest construction contract you will ever sign. Florida requires contractors who build homes for others to hold a state license. The license shows the contractor has met the state's requirements and is accountable to a state licensing board.

It doesn't tell you whether a builder is a good fit, communicates well, or prices honestly. It is the baseline. A builder who can't clear it shouldn't be on your list.

Step-by-Step: Verifying a License on the DBPR Site

1. Get the license number from the builder

Ask for it directly. A legitimate builder will give it to you without hesitation. Florida contractors are generally expected to include their license number on contracts and advertising, so check the website, proposal, or business card too.

If a builder dodges the question or offers someone else's license number, take note.

2. Run the search

On myfloridalicense.com, use the option to verify or search for a licensee. You can search by:

  • License number. This is the most precise option.
  • Individual name. Use this for the licensed contractor.
  • Business name. Use this for the company.

Searching by license number avoids confusion with similarly named people or companies. Construction contractors are licensed under the Construction Industry Licensing Board, so narrow the search to that board if the site asks.

3. Read the license type

Florida issues several kinds of contractor licenses. Those most relevant to building a home include:

  • General contractor
  • Building contractor
  • Residential contractor

Each type has its own scope of work, and some come with limits on the kind or size of structure. Don't assume. If you aren't sure the license covers your project, ask the builder to explain the scope in writing. You can also contact DBPR directly.

4. Check certified vs. registered

Florida licenses are either certified or registered:

  • Certified licenses generally allow a contractor to work anywhere in the state.
  • Registered licenses are tied to specific local jurisdictions.

If a builder holds a registered license, confirm it is valid where your lot is. That might be the City of Orlando, Winter Park, Lake Mary, unincorporated Orange County, or another jurisdiction.

5. Confirm the status

The search result shows the license status. You want it to read as current and active. Anything else, such as delinquent, inactive, suspended, or revoked, should stop you until the builder gives a clear explanation you can check.

Look at the expiration date too. If renewal is coming up soon, ask about it.

6. Match the names

This step is easy to skip, and it matters. Florida generally requires a construction company to be qualified by a licensed individual, often called the qualifier.

Check that:

  • The company name on your contract matches a business connected to the license.
  • The licensed individual is actually involved with the company you are hiring.
  • The entity you pay is the entity that is licensed.

If the contract names one company and the license belongs to another, ask why before you go further.

7. Look for complaints or discipline

The DBPR licensee record may show public complaints or disciplinary actions. Not every builder with a complaint is a bad builder, and not every problem ends up there. If something appears, read it and ask the builder about it. A straight answer tells you a lot.

What the License Lookup Won't Tell You

A clean license is a starting point. These questions fill the gaps. Ask them out loud and get the important answers in writing.

Insurance

Ask for current certificates of insurance, including general liability and workers' compensation coverage as it applies. Check that the named insured matches the company on your contract. Ask how the builder confirms that their subcontractors are insured as well.

Permits

Ask who pulls the building permit. The licensed contractor responsible for the work typically does. Permits for a home on your lot go through the local building department, which may be the city or the county depending on where the lot sits.

If your lot is inside Orlando city limits, the City of Orlando website is where to start on permitting questions. If it is in Winter Park, Lake Mary, Windermere, or an unincorporated area, contact that city or county's building department. Requirements change, so confirm current rules with the city, county, or HOA that has jurisdiction over your lot.

Code and storm readiness

Central Florida homes are built to the Florida Building Code, which covers wind resistance and other hazards specific to the state. Ask the builder how their process handles code requirements and inspections. Ask them to walk you through what that means for your design, in plain terms.

Pricing and change orders

This is where many homeowners get surprised. Ask:

  • What is included in the price, and what is an allowance?
  • How are change orders priced and approved?
  • Will you see a change order in writing, with its cost, before work proceeds?
  • What happens if an allowance runs over?

A builder who answers these clearly before the contract will probably communicate clearly during the build.

Schedule and communication

No honest builder can promise a fixed move-in date. Weather, inspections, materials, and decisions all affect timing. What you can ask is:

  • How is the schedule built, and how often is it updated?
  • Who is your main point of contact?
  • How and how often will you hear about progress?

Lot fit

If you already own a lot, ask the builder to review it before design gets far along. Setbacks, drainage, utilities, and deed or HOA restrictions can all shape what you can build. If your lot is in Orange County, pull your parcel information from the Orange County Property Appraiser. Confirm restrictions with your HOA and the local jurisdiction, not from memory or a listing sheet.

If you are still looking for a lot, ask whether the builder will help you evaluate candidates before you buy.

References and past work

Ask to see completed homes and to speak with past clients. Pay attention to what they say about communication, budget, and how problems were handled. Every build has problems. What matters is how they get solved.

Red Flags to Watch For

Slow down if you see any of these:

  • The builder won't provide a license number, or the number doesn't come up in the DBPR search.
  • The license is inactive, delinquent, or belongs to a different company.
  • You are asked to pay a large deposit before a written contract is signed.
  • You are asked to pull the permit yourself as an "owner-builder" when you are hiring a contractor to build the home.
  • Pricing is vague, or change orders are handled with a handshake.
  • Answers to direct questions keep shifting.

None of these is automatically disqualifying in every situation. Each one deserves a clear explanation before you sign.

A Simple Pre-Signing Checklist

Before you sign with any builder, you should be able to check off each of these:

  1. License verified on the DBPR site as current and active.
  2. License type appropriate for your project.
  3. License valid for your lot's jurisdiction.
  4. Company on the contract matches the licensed business.
  5. Current certificates of insurance in hand.
  6. Clear written answer on who pulls permits.
  7. Written explanation of allowances and change order pricing.
  8. A named point of contact and a communication plan.
  9. Conversations with past clients.
